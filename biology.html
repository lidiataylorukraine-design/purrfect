1. Evolution

Domestic cats descend primarily from the African and Near Eastern wildcat (Felis lybica). Their domestication began approximately 10,000–12,000 years ago in the Fertile Crescent. As humans developed agricultural settlements, stored grain attracted rodents. Wildcats followed these rodents into human settlements, providing natural pest control. Humans benefited from their presence, while cats gained access to a reliable food source. This relationship gradually developed into domestication. Unlike dogs, which underwent extensive selective breeding for different working roles, domestic cats retained many characteristics of their wild ancestors for thousands of years. Modern selective breeding eventually produced numerous recognized breeds, including Persians, Siamese, Maine Coons, and British Shorthairs. Despite their differences in appearance, domestic cats share much of the same underlying anatomy and physiology.

2. Anatomy

A cat's body is designed for flexibility, balance, and rapid movement. The Skeleton The feline skeleton provides structural support, protects internal organs, and allows movement. Important adaptations include: Flexible Spine: Cats have a highly mobile vertebral column that allows them to arch, stretch, and rotate their bodies. Floating Clavicles: Their small collarbones are not rigidly connected to other bones. This allows greater shoulder movement and helps cats pass through narrow spaces. Tail: The tail assists with balance and communication. Digitigrade Legs: Cats walk on their toes rather than placing their entire feet on the ground. Muscular System Cats possess powerful muscles suited to short bursts of intense activity. Their hind legs provide much of the force needed for jumping, while their flexible shoulders and forelimbs help them climb, land, and manipulate objects. Cats also possess retractable claws, which are useful for climbing, gripping surfaces, and capturing prey.

3. Sensory System

Cats possess five basic senses, but their sensitivity differs significantly from that of humans. Vision Cats are particularly well adapted to seeing in low-light conditions. Their eyes contain numerous rod photoreceptors, which are sensitive to dim light. They also possess a reflective layer called the tapetum lucidum, which reflects light back through the retina. This structure contributes to their ability to see in conditions where humans struggle. However, cats cannot see in complete darkness. Hearing Cats have highly developed hearing. Their ears can detect high-frequency sounds, including frequencies produced by small rodents. Their movable external ears help them locate the direction of sounds. This ability is especially useful when hunting. Smell A cat's sense of smell is significantly more developed than a human's. Cats use scent to recognize other animals, investigate unfamiliar environments, and communicate through chemical signals. They also possess a vomeronasal organ, sometimes called Jacobson's organ. When a cat opens its mouth slightly after encountering an interesting smell, it may be performing the Flehmen response, which helps transfer chemical signals to this organ. Taste Cats have taste receptors that detect several important taste categories. Interestingly, domestic cats lack a functional version of the receptor responsible for detecting sweetness. This is associated with their evolutionary specialization as carnivores. Touch Cats use specialized sensory hairs called vibrissae, commonly known as whiskers. These hairs are located around the muzzle, above the eyes, and in other areas. Whiskers detect nearby objects and movements through mechanical vibrations. They provide important information about the surrounding environment.

4. Nervous System

The nervous system controls and coordinates the activities of the feline body. It consists of the central nervous system, which includes the brain and spinal cord, and the peripheral nervous system, which connects the central nervous system to the rest of the body. The Brain The brain processes sensory information, controls movement, regulates internal functions, and contributes to learning and memory. Different regions perform specialized functions, including processing visual information, coordinating movement, and controlling automatic bodily processes. The Righting Reflex One of the most interesting feline abilities is the righting reflex. When falling, cats can use information from their inner ears and other sensory systems to orient their bodies. They rotate their head, adjust their spine, and reposition their legs. This ability develops during kittenhood. Although cats have impressive righting abilities, they are not immune to injuries from falls. Coordination The cerebellum is particularly important for balance and coordinated movement. It helps cats perform precise actions such as jumping, climbing, and walking along narrow surfaces.

5. Digestive System

Domestic cats are obligate carnivores, meaning their bodies have evolved to depend on nutrients naturally associated with animal tissues. Their digestive system processes food and absorbs nutrients needed for energy, growth, and maintenance. Teeth Cats have specialized teeth: Incisors help grasp and nibble food. Canines are used for gripping and tearing. Premolars and molars help cut and break food into smaller pieces. Stomach The stomach stores food and mixes it with digestive fluids. Acid and enzymes begin the process of breaking down proteins and other nutrients. Intestines The small intestine is responsible for absorbing most nutrients. The large intestine absorbs water and helps form solid waste. Nutritional Requirements Cats require several nutrients that their bodies cannot produce in sufficient quantities. These include: Taurine Arginine Arachidonic acid Preformed vitamin A Certain essential fatty acids These requirements explain why cats need nutritionally complete feline diets. Importantly, obligate carnivore does not mean cats must eat exclusively unprocessed meat. Properly formulated commercial cat foods can provide all the nutrients they require.

6. Respiratory System

The respiratory system supplies oxygen to the body and removes carbon dioxide. Air enters through the nose or mouth and travels through the respiratory tract. The main structures include: Nasal passages Pharynx Larynx Trachea Bronchi Lungs Inside the lungs are tiny structures called alveoli. These allow oxygen to enter the bloodstream while carbon dioxide moves from the blood into the air that will be exhaled. Purring Purring is one of the most recognizable feline vocalizations. Research suggests that the larynx plays an important role in producing the characteristic low-frequency sound. A 2023 scientific study demonstrated that isolated cat larynges could produce purring-like sounds without active neural input. However, the complete mechanism of purring in living cats is still being investigated. Thermoregulation and Breathing Cats have relatively few functional sweat glands distributed across their bodies compared with humans. They primarily regulate temperature through other mechanisms, including grooming and behavioral changes. Panting can also occur, particularly during heat or stress, although persistent panting in cats should be taken seriously.

8. Reproduction

Domestic cats have reproductive systems adapted to seasonal breeding. Female cats are known as queens, while males are commonly called toms. Seasonal Reproduction Female cats are generally seasonally polyestrous. This means they can experience multiple reproductive cycles during particular seasons, especially when daylight hours are increasing. Induced Ovulation Cats are generally induced ovulators. Unlike many mammals, female cats usually release eggs in response to hormonal changes triggered by mating. This process involves the release of luteinizing hormone (LH). Gestation Pregnancy in domestic cats generally lasts approximately 63–65 days. A litter commonly contains several kittens, although litter sizes vary. After birth, kittens depend on their mother for nourishment, warmth, and protection during their early development.

9. Genetics

Genetics determines many characteristics of domestic cats, including coat colour, fur patterns, eye colour, and certain inherited health conditions. A cat receives genetic information from both parents. Pigments Two major pigments contribute to feline coat colours: Eumelanin: Responsible for black and brown pigmentation. Phaeomelanin: Responsible for red and orange pigmentation. Different genes influence how these pigments are produced and distributed. The Orange Gene Orange coloration is associated with a gene located on the X chromosome. Because female cats usually have two X chromosomes, they can carry different colour variants on each chromosome. This helps explain why calico and tortoiseshell cats are overwhelmingly female. Male cats usually have only one X chromosome, so they typically express either orange or non-orange coloration. Tabby Patterns The agouti gene influences whether individual hairs contain alternating bands of pigment. Other genes influence the visible arrangement of tabby markings. Common patterns include: Mackerel Classic Spotted Ticked Colourpoint Patterns Siamese cats possess a temperature-sensitive form of the tyrosinase enzyme. This affects pigment production. Cooler areas of the body, such as the ears, paws, face, and tail, develop darker pigmentation than warmer areas. Inherited Conditions Certain genetic variants are associated with inherited health conditions. Examples include: Polycystic kidney disease (PKD), associated with certain Persian bloodlines. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), which occurs in several breeds, including Maine Coons. Genetic research helps scientists understand inheritance and develop better methods of identifying inherited conditions.

10. Urinary System

The urinary system removes metabolic waste products from the bloodstream and helps regulate the body's water and mineral balance. Its main components are: Kidneys Ureters Urinary bladder Urethra The Kidneys The kidneys filter blood and produce urine. They also regulate electrolyte concentrations and help maintain the body's fluid balance. Cats have a notable ability to produce relatively concentrated urine. This adaptation developed in their ancestors, which lived in environments where water availability could be limited. Urine Production Urine travels from the kidneys through the ureters into the urinary bladder. It is stored there until it leaves the body through the urethra. The urinary system is essential for maintaining internal chemical balance.

11. Immune System

The immune system protects cats against harmful microorganisms and other foreign substances. It consists of specialized cells, tissues, organs, and chemical processes. White Blood Cells White blood cells are essential components of immune defence. Different types perform different functions: Neutrophils help destroy invading microorganisms. Lymphocytes include cells responsible for antibody production and targeted immune responses. Monocytes can develop into macrophages, which engulf foreign particles and damaged cells. Lymphatic System The lymphatic system transports lymph, a fluid containing immune cells and other substances. Lymph nodes filter lymph and provide locations where immune cells can encounter foreign material. Innate and Adaptive Immunity The immune system has two major branches. Innate immunity provides rapid, general protection. Adaptive immunity develops more specific responses and can retain immunological memory. Together, these systems help protect cats from infections and disease.

12. Thermoregulation

Thermoregulation is the biological process of maintaining a suitable internal body temperature. Domestic cats generally maintain a body temperature of approximately 38.0–39.2°C. Their bodies use several mechanisms to regulate heat. Heat Loss Cats can lose heat through: Radiation Conduction Convection Evaporation Grooming contributes to evaporative cooling because saliva deposited on the fur can evaporate. Cats also have sweat glands on their paw pads, although these contribute relatively little to overall cooling. Behavioural Thermoregulation Cats frequently change their behaviour to regulate temperature. When warm, they may seek shaded areas, stretch out, or reduce activity. When cold, they may curl into a smaller shape or seek warm locations. These behaviours help maintain a stable internal temperature.
